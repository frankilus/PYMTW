Leaving a job? Don’t let your old 401(k) leak.

Your four options side by side, what cashing out really costs, and how to move the money without tax surprises. Every rule links to its source. Updated October 26, 2026.

After leaving a job, you can usually leave your 401(k) in the old plan, move it to a new employer’s plan, roll it to an IRA or Roth IRA, or cash it out.1 A direct rollover avoids the mandatory 20% withholding.2 Moving pre-tax money to a Roth IRA is taxable, and cashing out before 59½ can add a 10% tax.5

Your choices

Four doors. One is expensive.

1

The four things you can do with an old 401(k)

The IRS, FINRA and the SEC all describe the same four choices, and you can mix them (for example, roll most of it and keep a little in the plan).11516

Option 1Leave it in the old plan

Often allowed. Worth considering if you like the plan’s investments and fees are low. Small balances can be pushed out (step 3).1

Option 2Move it to your new employer’s plan

Only if the new plan accepts rollovers. Plans aren’t required to, so ask first.2 Keeps everything in one place.

Option 3Roll it to an IRA

A traditional IRA keeps pre-tax money tax-deferred. A Roth IRA is possible too, but the untaxed amount becomes income that year.1

Option 4Cash it out

The pre-tax amount is taxed as income, and before 59½ you may owe a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies.25

3

Leaving it: fine, if the plan lets you and you don’t lose track

  • Small balances can be pushed out. A plan may move a former employee’s small balance without consent. SECURE 2.0 lets plans raise that involuntary cash-out limit to $7,000, up from $5,000.11 The IRS says balances of $1,000 or less may simply be paid to you, in most cases minus 20% withholding, while larger amounts may go into an IRA opened in your name.2 You can still roll a payment over within 60 days.2
  • Old accounts get lost. The Department of Labor’s free Retirement Savings Lost and Found Database, created by SECURE 2.0, covers private-sector employer and union plans. It needs an ID-verified Login.gov account, and it can’t find IRAs or government plans.13
  • Keep your contact details current with every old plan, so statements and required notices reach you.
4

IRA, Roth IRA or new plan: match the money to its tax type

  • Pre-tax money → traditional IRA or new plan: no tax now.1
  • Pre-tax money → Roth IRA: allowed, but the untaxed amount is added to your income for the year, like a Roth conversion.13 A year with a lower income, such as a gap between jobs, can make that tax bill smaller, because only the income in a higher bracket is taxed at the higher rate.17
  • Roth 401(k) money → Roth IRA: allowed, Roth to Roth.3
  • After-tax (non-Roth) contributions → Roth IRA: IRS Notice 2014-54 lets you split one distribution: pre-tax amounts (including earnings on after-tax money) to a traditional IRA, and the after-tax contributions to a Roth IRA. You can’t pull out only the after-tax part and leave the rest in the plan.67

    Example: cashing out $50,000 at age 40

    An illustration, not a prediction: someone age 40 cashes out a $50,000 pre-tax 401(k) after leaving a job, in the 22% federal bracket with a 5% state rate, and no exception applies.25

    • The check is not the bill. The plan must withhold 20%, so the check would be about $40,000.2 The $10,000 withheld counts toward your federal tax, and the rest is settled on your tax return.

    7

    The Rule of 55: the one reason to keep money in the old plan

    If you leave your employer during or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer’s plan are not hit with the 10% additional tax. For public safety employees of a state or local government in a governmental plan, the age is 50, and the IRS also extends it to certain federal law enforcement officers, firefighters and air traffic controllers.5

    55the calendar year you turn 55 or later, when you leave the job.
    That planonly the plan of the employer you just left.
    Not IRAsroll the money to an IRA and you wait until 59½ or need another exception.

    Income tax still applies to pre-tax money. If you’re 55 to 59½ and might need some of the cash, consider leaving at least that part in the plan before rolling the rest.514

    Do it right

    Move the money without tax surprises.

    8

    Direct vs. indirect rollover: always ask for direct

    Direct rollover

    • The plan sends the money to your new plan or IRA, or mails a check payable to the new account.
    • No tax withheld.
    • No 60-day clock for you.24

    Indirect (60-day) rollover

    • The plan pays you and must withhold 20%, even if you plan to roll it over.
    • You have 60 days to deposit it.
    • To roll over the full amount you must add the withheld 20% from other money, or that part is taxed and possibly penalized.24
    • The IRS example: on a $10,000 distribution with $2,000 withheld, rolling over only $8,000 makes the $2,000 taxable income (plus the 10% additional tax if under 59½). Adding $2,000 of your own money makes the whole rollover tax-free.2
    • The one-rollover-per-year rule doesn’t apply here. It limits 60-day IRA-to-IRA rollovers. Plan-to-IRA rollovers, plan-to-plan rollovers and direct trustee-to-trustee transfers aren’t counted.2
    • Missed the 60 days? The IRS may waive the deadline for circumstances beyond your control.2
    9

    Owe a 401(k) loan? Know the offset rule

    Plan loan terms often require the loan to be repaid right away, or treat it as in default, when you leave the job. If it isn’t, the plan can reduce (offset) your account by the unpaid amount, and that offset counts as a distribution, taxable unless you roll it over.8

    • When the offset happens because you left the job, it’s usually a qualified plan loan offset. You then have until your tax filing due date, including extensions, for that year to roll over the offset amount using other money.48
    • You can’t move the loan itself into an IRA. IRAs don’t permit loans.9
    10

    Hold company stock in the plan? Pause before rolling it

    If a lump-sum distribution includes your employer’s stock, the net unrealized appreciation (NUA, the growth while it was in the plan) is generally not taxed until you sell the shares.10 FINRA explains that this usually means taking all of the plan in one lump sum and moving the shares into a regular, taxable account. You pay ordinary income tax on the cost basis now, and the growth is taxed later as a long-term capital gain.14 Rolling the shares into an IRA gives up that treatment.10 This is worth a conversation with a tax professional before you sign anything.

That 1 point difference cuts the balance at retirement by 28%.12 Compare the old plan, the new plan and any IRA on total cost before you move anything.

    Step-by-step rollover checklist

    1. Find every old account. Dig up old statements, or search the Department of Labor’s Lost and Found database.13
    2. Get the facts from the old plan. Your vested balance, how much is pre-tax, Roth and after-tax, any loan balance, any company stock, and the fees you pay.12
    3. Choose where it goes, and set that up first. Open the IRA, or confirm the new employer’s plan accepts rollovers and what types.2
    4. Request a direct rollover. Use the old plan’s forms. If they mail a check, it should be payable to the new account, not to you. Your new provider will give you the exact payee name and account number to use.2
    5. Send each type of money to the right place. Pre-tax to a traditional IRA or plan, Roth 401(k) money to a Roth IRA, and after-tax contributions to a Roth IRA if you split the distribution.36
    6. Confirm it arrived and is invested. Check the new account, and pick your investments if the money lands in cash.
    7. Keep the paperwork for taxes. A rollover isn’t taxable (unless it goes to a Roth IRA from pre-tax money), but it is still reported on your federal tax return.4
    8. Name your beneficiaries on the new account.

    Common questions

    What are my options for a 401(k) when I leave a job?

    You can usually leave the money in your old employer’s plan, roll it into your new employer’s plan if that plan accepts rollovers, roll it into a traditional or Roth IRA, or cash it out. You can also combine options. Cashing out is usually the most expensive choice because of income tax and, before 59½, a possible 10% additional tax.

    Do I pay taxes when I roll over my 401(k)?

    Not if you move pre-tax money directly into a traditional IRA or another employer plan. Ask for a direct rollover so nothing is withheld. Rolling pre-tax money into a Roth IRA is different: the untaxed amount counts as income for that year.

    How long do I have to roll over a 401(k)?

    With a direct rollover, the plan sends the money straight to the new account, so there is no 60-day clock for you to meet. If the plan pays you instead, you generally have 60 days from the day you receive it to deposit it into an IRA or another plan. The plan must withhold 20% on that check, so you need other money to roll over the full amount.

    Can I roll my 401(k) into a Roth IRA?

    Yes. Roth 401(k) money can roll into a Roth IRA. Pre-tax money can too, but the untaxed amount is added to your income for the year, so it works like a Roth conversion. If your plan has after-tax contributions, IRS rules let you send those to a Roth IRA and the pre-tax part to a traditional IRA in the same distribution.

    What happens if I cash out my 401(k) before 59½?

    The pre-tax amount is taxed as ordinary income, and you may owe a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies, such as leaving your job in or after the year you turn 55. In this guide’s example, cashing out $50,000 at age 40 with a 22% federal and 5% state rate leaves about $31,500.

    What is the Rule of 55 for 401(k) withdrawals?

    If you leave your employer during or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer’s plan are not subject to the 10% additional tax. For qualified public safety employees, the age is 50. It does not apply to IRAs, so rolling the money to an IRA gives up this exception for that money.

    Can my old employer force my 401(k) out of the plan?

    For small balances, yes, if the plan has that rule. SECURE 2.0 lets plans raise the involuntary cash-out limit to $7,000, up from $5,000. The IRS says balances of $1,000 or less may simply be paid to you, usually with 20% withheld, while larger small balances may be moved to an IRA in your name if you don’t choose.

    Does the one-rollover-per-year rule apply to 401(k) rollovers?

    No. The IRS one-rollover-per-12-months limit applies to 60-day rollovers from one IRA to another. It does not apply to rollovers from a 401(k) to an IRA, plan-to-plan rollovers, or direct trustee-to-trustee transfers.
