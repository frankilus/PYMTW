Just lost your job? Here’s what to do.

A calm, plain-English plan for this week, this month, and this year. Most of the deadlines that matter come up in the first 60 days.

After losing your job, file for unemployment right away, work out how many months your money will last, review your severance and final paycheck, and choose health coverage (COBRA or a Marketplace plan) within the 60-day window. Then trim your budget and avoid cashing out your 401(k) if you can.

This week

Steady the ground.

1

File for unemployment as soon as you can

Unemployment insurance is run by each state, so the rules, amounts and how long benefits last depend on where you worked. You generally file with the state where you worked, online, by phone or in person. The U.S. Department of Labor says to contact your state’s program as soon as possible after you become unemployed, and that it generally takes two to three weeks after filing to get your first payment.

  • You usually qualify if you lost your job through no fault of your own and meet your state’s work and wage requirements.
  • How much you get and for how long varies by state. Many states pay up to 26 weeks, and some pay fewer. Check your state’s rules with the CareerOneStop finder below.
  • Have your past employers’ names, addresses and dates of work ready. The DOL says incomplete information can delay a claim.
  • Find your state’s filing site with the CareerOneStop Unemployment Benefits Finder (sponsored by the U.S. Department of Labor).

Source: DOL: How do I file for unemployment insurance?

3

Read your severance agreement and check your final paycheck

Federal law doesn’t require employers to hand over your final paycheck immediately, but some states do. If your regular payday for your last pay period has passed and you haven’t been paid, the DOL suggests contacting its Wage and Hour Division or your state labor department.

  • Before you sign a severance agreement, note the deadline, payment timing, any release of claims, and non-compete or non-disparagement terms. Many people have an employment attorney review it.
  • Ask HR about unused vacation pay (the rules vary by state and employer), your last day of health coverage, and whether the company will pay any of your COBRA premium.

Source: DOL: Last paycheck

4

Call lenders before you miss a payment

Many lenders and card issuers have hardship programs, but you usually have to ask. If you have federal student loans, ask your servicer about income-driven repayment or a temporary pause.

Lock in health coverage. Trim the budget.

5

Choose between COBRA and a Marketplace plan, within 60 days

You have two main paths. You don’t have to take COBRA, and HealthCare.gov suggests comparing its cost with Marketplace plans before you decide.

60 daysto elect COBRA, from when coverage ends or your notice arrives, whichever is later
60 daysto enroll in a Marketplace plan after losing job-based coverage
Up to 102%of the plan’s cost is what COBRA may charge you

COBRA

  • Keeps your exact plan, doctors and deductible progress.
  • You usually pay the full premium plus 2%.
  • Usually lasts up to 18 months after a job loss.
  • Generally applies to employers with 20 or more employees. Many states have “mini-COBRA” laws for smaller employers.
  • After you elect it, you have 45 days to make the first payment, and coverage is retroactive.

ACA Marketplace

  • Losing job-based coverage opens a Special Enrollment Period.
  • You may qualify for savings based on your income, which may be lower now.
  • If you qualify for Medicaid or CHIP, you can enroll at any time.
  • Careful: dropping COBRA early on your own doesn’t open a new enrollment window. You’d usually wait for Open Enrollment.
  • If you have a spouse or parent with a job-based plan, you may be able to join it. The DOL says you generally need to request this within 30 days of losing your coverage.

Sources: DOL: FAQs on COBRA for workers · DOL: COBRA · HealthCare.gov: COBRA when you’re unemployed

This year

Protect the long game.

7

Decide what to do with your 401(k), and avoid cashing out if you can

You don’t usually have to decide right away. Common options:

  • Leave it in your old plan, if the plan allows (many do above a minimum balance).
  • Roll it into an IRA or into your new employer’s plan. A direct rollover (plan to plan, or plan to IRA) has no taxes withheld.
  • Cash it out. If the money is paid to you, the plan must withhold 20%, even if you plan to roll it over. You have 60 days to deposit it into an IRA or plan, and you’d need other money to replace the withheld 20%. Whatever you don’t roll over is generally taxable, and before age 59½ it may also face a 10% additional tax.
  • There are exceptions to the 10% tax. One applies if you leave your employer at age 55 or older and take money from that employer’s plan. IRS Topic 558 has the exact timing rules, so check them or ask a tax professional before you rely on it.

Sources: IRS: Rollovers of retirement plan and IRA distributions · IRS Topic 558: Additional tax on early distributions

8

Plan for taxes on unemployment benefits

Unemployment benefits are generally taxable income. You can ask to have federal income tax withheld (Form W-4V), or you may need to make estimated payments. You’ll get a Form 1099-G showing what you were paid.

Source: IRS Topic 418: Unemployment compensation

Common questions

What should I do first after being laid off?

File for unemployment with your state as soon as you can, then check your final paycheck and any severance terms, and figure out how long your savings will last.

Should I choose COBRA or a Marketplace plan?

COBRA keeps your current plan but you usually pay the full premium. Losing job-based coverage also opens a special enrollment period for a Marketplace plan, which may cost less if you qualify for savings. Compare both before your deadlines.

Should I cash out my 401(k) after losing my job?

Usually it is better not to. Money you don’t roll over is generally taxable, and before age 59½ it may also face a 10% additional tax, though there are exceptions.

Are unemployment benefits taxable?

Generally yes, for federal income tax. You can ask to have tax withheld from your benefits to avoid a surprise bill.
