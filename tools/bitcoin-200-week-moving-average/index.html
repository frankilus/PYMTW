These are observations, not odds. Consecutive weeks overlap. One market cycle can appear many times, and a small number of cycles cannot establish what happens next.

Each eligible observation has 52 subsequent weekly samples. “Worst drop along the way” is the largest fall from a starting observation to any sampled price in the following 52 weeks; intraday losses may have been larger. “Observed stretches” groups consecutive eligible observations in the same range; these stretches are not independent trials. Sources & methodology+

Sources and method

Historical series. Blockchain.com’s daily average USD price across major exchanges, sampled on Sundays from 2010-08-22. A Sunday sample is not an exchange’s weekly closing price. The scheduled updater checks for new data daily.

Current reading. The average uses the latest 200 weekly observations, with the current, still-forming week represented by the newest snapshot. A live Coinbase quote may differ from the exchange-average history. If the live request fails, the page clearly labels its saved-price fallback. Quotes are fetched once per page load, not streamed continuously.

Historical comparisons. These use saved Sunday observations only, excluding the newest saved day. No live quote is included. Relative-price bands use rounded, descriptive thresholds of 1.1×, 2× and 3.5×; they are not fair-value estimates. Historical samples overlap and do not establish future probabilities.

Common questions+

What is the bitcoin 200-week moving average?

It is the plain average of bitcoin’s last 200 weekly prices, about 3.8 years of data. Because it moves slowly, it shows bitcoin’s long-run trend and smooths out the big rallies and crashes. This tracker divides today’s price by that average to give a multiple: 1.0x means price equals the average, 2.0x means price is double it.

Is bitcoin a buy when it falls below the 200-week moving average?

Not automatically. Historically, bitcoin has spent only a small share of weeks near or below this line, and those stretches have sat close to bear-market lows. But that is a pattern from a short history with a handful of separate episodes, and bitcoin has often kept falling or stayed low for months after touching it. Treat it as one piece of context for a plan you can stick to, not as a signal. This is education, not financial advice.

How are the colored zones decided?

The zones are rounded cut-offs on the price-to-average multiple: “Near or below average”: under 1.1x; “Above average”: 1.1x to under 2x; “Elevated”: 2x to under 3.5x; “Far above average”: 3.5x or more. They are a way to read the chart, not a forecast, and different people would draw the lines in different places.

Why is the chart on a log scale?

Bitcoin has gone from cents to tens of thousands of dollars. On a normal scale the early years would look flat. A log scale gives each doubling the same height, so a move from $1 to $2 looks the same as $30,000 to $60,000, and the zone bands keep a constant width.

Where does the data come from, and how current is it?

Weekly observations use the Blockchain.com daily average USD price sampled each Sunday, starting 2010-08-22; these are not weekly closing prices. A free scheduled GitHub Action checks for new history daily. On each page load, the tracker also requests one Coinbase Exchange price snapshot. If it is unavailable, the newest saved daily price is used and clearly labeled. The current snapshot is provisional. Historical outcome tables exclude the newest saved day and the live quote.

Does this tool give financial advice?

No. It describes where bitcoin’s price sits relative to its own history. It cannot tell you what bitcoin will do next, and past patterns may not repeat. Do not invest money you cannot afford to lose, and talk to a licensed professional before you make decisions.
