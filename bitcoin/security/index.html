YOUR NEXT GOOD STEP

Learn who can say “yes” to a withdrawal.

An exchange balance, a fund share and a wallet you control are different arrangements. Choose deliberately; self-custody is a responsibility, not an entrance exam.

  • Compare control and recovery before choosing a device.
  • Secure your email and financial accounts with strong, unique credentials and phishing-resistant authentication where supported.
  • Practice with an amount you can afford to lose before treating any new setup as savings.
Compare the six approaches ↓
YOUR NEXT GOOD STEP

Prove your recovery plan works.

Being able to open your app today does not tell you what happens if a device breaks, a company closes, or you are unavailable.

  • Identify who controls the keys and where the current backups are.
  • Use the maker’s verified backup-check flow; never erase your only working wallet to “test” recovery.
  • Review current advisories and leave non-secret directions for a trusted person.
Review the self-custody steps ↓
YOUR NEXT GOOD STEP

Name the failure you want to survive.

Theft of one key? Loss of a location? A provider disappearing? Design for that event, then rehearse the alternative path.

  • Keep independent keys and backups in genuinely separate places.
  • Preserve wallet configuration as well as the signing keys.
  • Practice recovery with a missing signer and check that your family can understand the plan.

02 / KNOW WHAT YOU OWN

Six approaches. Different responsibilities.

Open the options you want to compare. None removes Bitcoin’s price risk. More than one approach can serve different purposes.

01
Price exposure through a brokerage

ETF / ETP shares

You own fund shares. The fund’s custody arrangements handle the bitcoin.

+
What it does well
Convenient brokerage access; no personal seed phrase to manage.
What can go wrong
Product fees, market hours and fund/custodian risks remain. Ordinary retail shares generally cannot be withdrawn as bitcoin to your wallet.
When to consider it
You want price exposure and accept the product’s terms. This is not the same as controlling spendable bitcoin.
Before you choose
Read the prospectus, check custody and redemption terms, and secure your brokerage login.
[2]
02
A provider holds the keys

Exchange or custodian

You rely on the provider to process withdrawals.

+
What it does well
An accessible buying/selling route, account support and familiar login recovery.
What can go wrong
Hacks, insolvency, withdrawal freezes and account takeover. Account recovery is not a guarantee that assets can be recovered.
When to consider it
You accept third-party custody, or use an exchange as a short-term bridge while learning.
Before you choose
Check withdrawal support, legal terms, lending/commingling, insurance exclusions and account recovery. Protect the linked email.
[1]
03
You control keys on a phone or computer

Software wallet

A self-custody hot wallet can authorize spending from a connected device.

+
What it does well
Convenient for learning and everyday payments; direct access without asking a custodian.
What can go wrong
Device malware, fake apps and exposed backups can compromise access. A phone unlock code is not a recovery plan.
When to consider it
You need a spending wallet and keep exposure within a loss you can absorb.
Before you choose
Use verified software, protect the device, understand its backup, and keep spending funds separate from longer-term savings.
[3][1]
04
You control keys on a dedicated device

Hardware wallet · one signing key

The device signs; a connected app prepares and broadcasts transactions.

+
What it does well
Separates signing secrets from a general-purpose computer. Often a manageable long-term setup.
What can go wrong
Bad seed generation, compromised backups, misleading prompts, physical access and loss still matter. The device’s brand is not a guarantee.
When to consider it
You can maintain an offline backup, verify transactions and rehearse recovery.
Before you choose
Buy directly from the manufacturer’s official website, then follow the current model-specific authenticity and setup checks. Check advisories and test recovery before relying on it. A well-run single-key setup may be sufficient.
[3][15]
05
You and a service hold different keys

Collaborative custody

The signing threshold—not the marketing label—determines who can spend.

+
What it does well
Can combine professional help with an independent recovery route. In one 2-of-3 model, you hold two keys and the provider holds one.
What can go wrong
Ongoing fees, identity/privacy requirements, provider availability and contract terms. Some arrangements require a provider to sign.
When to consider it
You want support and understand exactly what happens if you or the provider become unavailable.
Before you choose
Ask who can meet the threshold, how to exit without the provider, what configuration you receive, and how heirs recover. Do not generalize one provider’s model to all services.
[11][12]
06
Several independent keys; you run the system

DIY multisignature

For example, any two of three independent keys can authorize a spend.

+
What it does well
Can survive one lost or compromised key when locations and access are properly separated.
What can go wrong
More devices, configuration and operational work. Two keys together can defeat the intended separation; loss of the threshold can lock you out.
When to consider it
You have a specific single-point-of-failure concern and can operate and recover the arrangement reliably.
Before you choose
Back up the threshold, key order/policy, extended public keys, fingerprints and derivation information in a compatible wallet configuration/descriptor. Protect it as privacy-sensitive data.
[10][12]
What about Lightning, wrapped bitcoin and other “Bitcoin” products?

Lightning wallets can be custodial or self-custodial; channel backups, liquidity and offline-recovery needs vary. A Lightning spending balance is not automatically the same arrangement as an on-chain savings wallet. Read the exact wallet’s recovery documentation.

A token representing bitcoin on another chain, lending account, derivative or mining-company share adds issuer, bridge, contract, credit or business risks. Do not infer direct bitcoin control from the name or ticker. Compare the legal and technical arrangement before using it.

03 / IF YOU CHOOSE SELF-CUSTODY

Go slowly.
Make recovery part
of the setup.

For setting up a new hardware wallet: a device that keeps your signing keys separate from your phone or computer. Follow these steps alongside the maker’s official guide for your exact model.

Stop if anything does not match. Already have bitcoin in a wallet? Do not reset it or start again with this checklist. Verify your backup and check the maker’s current security notices before making changes.

Optional review checklist · nothing is saved.
  1. 01

    Buy directly from the manufacturer.

    Buy a new device only from the maker’s official website. Avoid resellers, Amazon, other marketplaces and used devices—even if advertised as “official” or “sealed.”

    Before you continue: Check the website address carefully. Buying direct still requires the device checks in step 2.

    Compare hardware wallets ↗
  2. 02

    Check the device before setting it up.

    Open the maker’s official setup guide for your exact model. Use its official app, check that the device is genuine, and follow its instructions for installing or updating firmware—the software inside the device.

    Before you continue: Check current security notices. Stop if a seal, device check or warning does not match the guide. A sealed box alone does not prove the device is safe.

    Already use COLDCARD? Read its current security notice ↗[8]
  3. 03

    Create a new wallet on the device.

    Follow the maker’s “new wallet” setup and set a PIN when prompted. The PIN locks the device. The device should generate your recovery words—the private backup used to restore access if the device is lost.

    Before you continue: Never make up your own recovery words or use words supplied with the device. Keep the device private while setting it up.

    Understand recovery words ↗[4]
  4. 04

    Write down the recovery words privately.

    Copy every word in the exact order shown, using the maker’s backup instructions. Keep this physical backup in a secure place, separate from the device. Consider protection from fire and water.

    Before you continue: No photos, email, cloud notes or AI uploads. Do not share the words. They are much more sensitive than the device’s PIN.

    How to protect your backup ↗[4]
  5. 05

    Check that your backup is correct.

    If your device has an official “check backup” feature, follow the instructions for your model. This checks your written words against the wallet without deleting it. If it has no such feature, use the maker’s official guidance before funding it.

    Before you continue: Do not wipe a funded device to practice. Never enter recovery words into this website, a web form, chat or unsolicited support prompt.

    See a backup-check example for Trezor Model T ↗[5]
  6. 06

    Receive a small test payment.

    In the wallet app, choose Bitcoin and “Receive.” Copy the receiving address into the exchange or wallet you are sending from. Compare the entire pasted address with the address on your hardware device’s screen.

    Before you continue: Choose the Bitcoin network (on-chain), check the amount and fee, then send only a small amount you can afford to lose. Wait until it is confirmed in the intended wallet before sending more. Recheck every later transfer.

    See how to receive and verify an address ↗[6]
  7. 07

    Practice sending a small amount.

    Choose a receiving wallet or account you control. In your hardware-wallet app, select “Send” and enter its Bitcoin address and a small test amount. Check the destination address, amount and fee on the hardware device before approving.

    Before you continue: Wait for confirmation at the destination. You should understand both receiving and sending before using this setup for substantial savings.

    Learn to check a transaction on the device ↗[15]
  8. 08

    Leave a recovery plan for your family.

    Write down the wallet type, official recovery guide and who can help. Plan how a trusted person could securely reach the backup if you were unavailable. Keep recovery words out of ordinary shared documents.

    Before you continue: Set a review reminder. Check security notices, the backup’s condition and your family instructions after a move, new device or major life change.

04 / CHANGE FOR A REASON

Better security isn’t a bigger key collection.

There is no universal bitcoin balance or dollar threshold for an upgrade. Consider what a loss would do to your life—and whether you can reliably operate the alternative.

FROM SPENDING TO SAVINGS

When a phone-sized risk becomes a household-sized risk.

If losing the hot-wallet balance would affect essentials or long-term plans, consider separating daily spending from a hardware-wallet savings setup. First establish a private backup location, practice address verification, and check recovery.

FROM ONE KEY TO SEVERAL

When one compromised key or location is an unacceptable failure.

Consider multisig or a collaborative model only if independent locations, compatible signers, configuration backups and a missing-key rehearsal are realistic. Keeping every key in the same drawer does not solve location risk.

FROM “I KNOW HOW” TO A FAMILY PLAN

When other people depend on access.

Plan now, at any balance. Document the recovery route and trusted contacts. Separate ordinary instructions from the secrets, and rehearse with a small practice wallet. Technical access and legal authority are different; involve a qualified estate professional where appropriate.

Before adding a passphrase, seed shares, air gaps or a node

A passphrase is not your PIN.

A BIP-39 passphrase creates a different wallet with the same seed. A typo can open another, empty wallet; losing the exact passphrase can make funds unrecoverable. Back it up separately and test the complete recovery. It is an optional advanced layer, not an automatic beginner requirement.

[7]

Splitting a backup is not multisig.

A standardized threshold-backup scheme reconstructs one secret; multisig requires signatures from independent keys. Do not invent a system by cutting a recovery phrase into pieces. Check format compatibility and recovery tooling before relying on either.

[10][4]

An air gap is a workflow, not immunity.

QR codes and memory cards still carry data. Read and verify what the trusted display asks you to authorize. Keep firmware provenance and recovery checks in the plan.

[15]

A node helps verification and privacy.

It does not rescue a stolen seed or a mistaken approval. Watch-only wallets cannot sign, but exposed extended public keys/configuration can reveal wallet activity. Treat them as privacy-sensitive.

[14]
A useful upgrade test

Can I explain the new failure modes, recover without one component, and leave understandable instructions? If not, improve the current setup before moving meaningful funds to the new one.

05 / CURRENT GUIDANCE, NOT OLD ASSUMPTIONS

A fix is not always
a recovery plan.

Primary-source review · 3 November 2026

COLDCARD’s affected-seed guidance

The manufacturer’s 2026 guidance distinguishes fixed firmware from an already affected seed. It says to create and verify a new seed and migrate affected funds; an update alone does not repair the old seed. Scope depends on the original generation flow and firmware track, not just today’s hardware model.

A strong passphrase does not remove the migration requirement. The advisory has precise independent-dice exceptions; do not assume any added dice or a later import makes a seed safe.

Manufacturer statements, not a PYMTW security audit. Check the live advisory before acting; this page does not automatically monitor incidents or firmware. Do not erase your only working wallet or improvise a migration.

Lessons that apply beyond one device

Check how keys were created, what the device is signing, and whether backups recover the expected wallet. A firmware fix, a strong brand, an air gap or a support team cannot substitute for all three.

Fake support and account phishing are separate risks. Start from bookmarked official sites; use supported phishing-resistant sign-in methods, and keep account recovery protected too.

06 / IF SOMETHING FEELS WRONG

Pause the risky action.
Choose the right response.

Never share recovery words or pay an unsolicited “recovery agent.” PYMTW has no access to your funds and cannot reverse a confirmed Bitcoin transaction.

I think someone has my recovery words or passphrase.

Treat that wallet as potentially compromised. A new PIN, reinstall, or another device using the same exposed recovery secret does not revoke the old keys. Use a trusted, clean environment and the maker’s verified process to establish new, independent recovery material; verify the destination and test before moving remaining funds. If a theft may be active, seek verified specialist help promptly without disclosing secrets. Migration decisions can be urgent, but unfamiliar signing prompts make them more dangerous.

[4][9]
My device is lost or broken, but my backup is private.

Do not assume the bitcoin disappeared with the hardware. Use official instructions for a compatible replacement and your exact backup format, passphrase and wallet policy. If theft or secret exposure is possible, reassess that risk separately. Never test recovery in a website, chat, or unverified app.

[5][7]
My exchange or email account may be compromised.

Use a known official route to the provider. Secure the linked email, revoke unauthorized sessions and follow the provider’s incident and withdrawal-lock procedures. Check account-recovery methods and authentication devices. Preserve relevant records; contact appropriate authorities if funds were stolen. Do not trust support accounts that contact you first.

[1][13]
A website asks me to “verify” my wallet with recovery words.

Stop. Do not type them, scan a secret-key QR, share your screen, or install remote-access software. Disconnect from the interaction. If you already disclosed a secret, use the compromised-secret response above rather than assuming closing the page fixes it.

[4]
